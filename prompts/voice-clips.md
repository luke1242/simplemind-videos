## Part 2

**part2a.mp3** (169 words)
```
[calm, storytelling] For a long time, people thought procrastination was a time problem. Bad planning. Not enough discipline. [curious] But psychologist Tim Pychyl has studied procrastination for decades, and he says something different. [emphatic] Procrastination isn't a time management problem. It's an emotion management problem. [thoughtful] In two thousand thirteen, he and psychologist Fuschia Sirois described it as choosing to feel better right now, at the expense of your future self. [calm] Think about how a task makes you feel. Boring. Confusing. Scary. Too big. [softly] Your brain doesn't like that feeling. So it looks for a way out.

[explaining] And the second you avoid the task, you get a little reward. Relief. [calm] Close the laptop, and the bad feeling is gone. Instantly. [serious] And your brain remembers that. Every time you avoid something hard and feel better, your brain learns, "Avoiding works." [thoughtful] So the next time, it reaches for that escape even faster. [slow] That's why procrastination can feel almost automatic. You're not deciding to be lazy. [softly] You're following a loop your brain learned to keep you comfortable.
```

**part2b.mp3** (172 words)
```
[storytelling] And there's another piece to this. [wry] So when you say, "I'll do it tomorrow," part of your brain hears, "Someone else will do it." [light] And that someone else is always more motivated, more rested, and more organized than you. [slow] Until tomorrow comes. And they turn out to be you.

[slow] So put it together. [thoughtful] Procrastination isn't laziness. It's your brain choosing to feel better now, and sending the bill to a version of you it doesn't really see as you. [curious] But why does it get so stuck? Why do some tasks sit there for days, or weeks, or even years?

## Part 3

**part3a.mp3** (100 words)
```
[calm] Researchers keep finding the same few traps. Here are the four that show up the most.

[explaining] One. The task is too big and too blurry. [calm] "Work on the project." "Get in shape." "Figure out my life." [thoughtful] Your brain can't see where to start, so it all feels like one giant, heavy thing. [storytelling] Back in nineteen eighty-four, psychologists Laura Solomon and Esther Rothblum asked hundreds of college students why they put things off. [emphatic] One of the biggest reasons was simply that the task felt unpleasant to face. [light] And nothing feels worse to face than a task with no clear first step.
```

**part3b.mp3** (88 words)
```
[explaining] Two. The task feels like a test of who you are. [calm] The other big reason in that same study was fear of failure. [thoughtful] If you never really start, you can never really fail. [softly] As long as it's unfinished, you can still tell yourself, "I could have done it great if I'd had more time." [serious] So the delay protects you. Not from the work... from finding out what you're capable of. [light] That's why the thing you care about most is so often the thing you put off the longest.
```

**part3c.mp3** (131 words)
```
[explaining] Three. Future you feels like a stranger. [calm] Remember those brain scans. [thoughtful] The reward for doing the task lives in the future. The bad feeling lives right now. [wry] And right now always wins, because right now is the only one your brain fully believes in. [calm] That's why a deadline three weeks away feels like nothing, and a deadline tomorrow feels like an emergency. [light] Same task. Different distance.

[explaining] Four. The guilt loop. [calm] You put something off. Then you feel bad about it. [serious] And feeling bad is exactly the kind of feeling your brain wants to escape. [thoughtful] So you avoid the task even harder, which makes you feel worse, which makes you avoid it even more. [slow] The shame doesn't push you forward. [softly] It becomes one more bad feeling standing between you and the work.
```

**part3d.mp3** (55 words)
```
[slow] So put those four together. A task that's too blurry. A task that feels like a test. A reward that feels too far away. And guilt that keeps feeding the loop. [thoughtful] That's not a lazy person. [warm] That's a brain trying to protect you, in a way that's quietly costing you the things you want most.

## Part 4

**part4a.mp3** (92 words)
```
[warm] So here's what actually works. Five steps.

[upbeat] One. Shrink it until it's almost silly. [calm] Don't "write the essay." Open the document and write one bad sentence. Don't "go to the gym." Just put your shoes on. [thoughtful] The goal isn't to finish. The goal is to make the first step so small that your brain has nothing to run from. [light] For this video, my first step wasn't "write a script." **part4b.mp3** (84 words)
```
[upbeat] Two. Make an if-then plan. [storytelling] Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer studied what he called implementation intentions. Instead of "I'll study this week," you decide exactly when and where. [calm] "If it's seven o'clock, then I sit at my desk and open my notes." [emphatic] When researchers combined ninety-four studies on this, if-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on people actually reaching their goals. [thoughtful] Because you're not waiting to feel like it anymore. The decision has already been made. All you have to do is show up for it.
